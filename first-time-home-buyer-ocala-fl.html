Guide

The Order of Operations

Most first-time buyers in Florida make the same mistake. They start looking at houses before they know what they can afford or what their loan options are, then fall for something the bank was never going to lend on.

Here is the right order.

One. Check your credit. Most conventional programs want 620 or better.

FHA, per HUD's published rules, allows 580 with 3.5% down. Pull your report at annualcreditreport.com and dispute anything wrong on it before you apply, because that takes time you will not have later.

Two. Get pre-approved. Not pre-qualified, which is a rough guess based on what you told someone.

Pre-approved means a lender verified your income, your assets, and your credit. Sellers in Ocala and Orlando expect that letter with any offer, and without it you are not really a buyer yet.

Three. Know your whole payment, not the mortgage. Property taxes, homeowners insurance, and any HOA dues or district assessment all land on top of principal and interest, and in Central Florida they add up to a meaningful share of what you pay every month. Get real quotes rather than working off a percentage you read somewhere.

Four. Now go look at houses. You will look at fewer of them and you will make better decisions about the ones you see, because you know what the true monthly number is instead of guessing.

Florida First-Time Buyer Programs Worth Knowing

Florida runs several programs built specifically to get first-time buyers past the down payment and closing cost hurdle.

Florida Housing Finance Corporation offers down payment assistance through participating lenders. The assistance amounts, the income limits, and the repayment terms are set by program and by county, and they get revised, so ask a participating lender what is currently available for your county and household rather than trusting a figure on any website, including this one.

Orange County and Marion County have each run their own local assistance programs in some years, depending on federal grant funding. These are easy to miss because they are not advertised well. Ask your lender directly.

FHA loans are the most common path for first-time buyers in Florida, at 3.5% down with a 580 score. Mortgage insurance comes with them and generally stays for the life of the loan on newer FHA loans, which is the tradeoff for the low barrier to entry.

VA loans require no down payment for eligible veterans and service members. If you have served, start here. It is usually worth looking at first.

USDA loans also require no down payment, on properties inside eligible areas. That matters a lot around Ocala, because much of Marion County outside the built-up core falls inside USDA-eligible territory. Eligibility is set address by address, so it has to be checked on the specific house.

Florida-Specific Costs First-Time Buyers Often Miss

A few costs here catch people off guard, especially buyers moving in from another state.

Homeowners insurance is the big one. Florida is among the most expensive states to insure a home in, and it got harder after 2022 as national carriers pulled back their exposure here. Roof age drives the quote more than almost anything else, so an otherwise fine house with a fifteen-year-old roof can come back with a premium that breaks your budget or with no offer to write at all.

Never make an offer without a real quote on that specific address. Flood insurance is separate and required in designated flood zones.

HOA dues are common in newer Central Florida developments and rarer in older Marion County subdivisions. What matters is not just the amount but what it covers and whether the association's reserves are funded, because an underfunded association eventually issues a special assessment and that lands on whoever owns the house at the time.

Then the state taxes on your loan, which almost nobody budgets for. Florida charges documentary stamp tax on the mortgage note at 35 cents per $100 of the amount financed, and a separate nonrecurring intangible tax on the mortgage at two mills, which is two dollars per thousand. Both are administered by the Florida Department of Revenue under Chapter 201 of the Florida Statutes.

Using a round number to show the math: on a $300,000 loan that is about $1,050 in documentary stamps plus about $600 in intangible tax. It shows up in your closing costs and it is not optional.

The homestead exemption works in your favor, and it is worth understanding properly because the version people repeat is wrong. Per the Florida Department of Revenue, the first $25,000 of the exemption applies to all property taxes including school district levies. An additional exemption applies to assessed value above $50,000, and that portion applies to every taxing authority except school districts, adjusted annually for inflation.

So it is not a flat reduction against everything. What it saves you in real dollars depends on the millage rate where the house sits, which is why your county property appraiser is the right place to get a number for a specific address instead of a rule of thumb. You apply after closing, and it also starts the Save Our Homes cap that limits how fast your assessed value can rise in later years.

Why Ocala Is Worth Considering for First-Time Buyers

The hardest part of buying your first home in Florida right now is not the loan. It is finding anything you can afford that you would actually want to live in.

That is the specific problem Marion County solves. Ocala still has genuine entry-level inventory, on real lots, which has become rare in this state. For a first-time buyer, that changes the question from whether you can find something to whether the tradeoffs work for you.

There is a second advantage that stacks on top and gets overlooked. Because much of the county sits inside USDA-eligible territory, a first-time buyer here has a realistic shot at a zero down payment purchase, which is not on the table in most of the Orlando metro. Low entry prices and a no-down-payment loan program in the same county is an unusual combination and it is the single strongest argument for looking here first.

The tradeoff is location, and I will not soft-pedal it. Ocala sits roughly 80 miles from downtown Orlando, which rules out a daily commute for almost everyone. If you work remotely, that is irrelevant.

If your job is here in healthcare, retail, or logistics, it is irrelevant. If you need to be in the metro five days a week, Ocala is the wrong answer and I will tell you that rather than sell you the drive.

I cover both Greater Orlando and Ocala. Once I know your situation, I can tell you which one your budget actually fits.
