Florida Hometown Heroes Program Explained

Down payment and closing cost help for people who work in Florida, structured as a second mortgage you repay later. Here is how the money works, who it covers, and why the funding question never has a permanent answer.

What This Program Actually Pays For

The Florida Hometown Heroes program is run by Florida Housing Finance Corporation, the state's housing finance agency, and it does one narrow job. It puts money toward the down payment and closing costs of a home purchase for first-time buyers working in a set of occupations the state decided it wanted to keep living here rather than commuting in from somewhere cheaper.

Florida Housing sets the assistance at up to 5% of the first mortgage loan amount, with a floor of $10,000 and a ceiling of $35,000.

Read that as a range with hard stops on both ends. A modest loan does not shrink your assistance below the minimum. A large one does not push it past the maximum. And because the calculation runs off the first mortgage amount rather than the sale price, two buyers on the same street can land on different numbers depending on how their financing is structured.

The money is for the down payment, closing costs, and prepaid items. Not a furniture budget, not a renovation fund, not a cushion sitting in your account the week after closing.

Now the part that gets skipped in almost every conversation I have about this program. It is not a grant. Florida Housing describes it as a 0%, non-amortizing, 30-year deferred second mortgage. Every word in that phrase is carrying weight, and the next section takes them apart one at a time, because a lien goes on your house and buyers should understand that before they sign rather than after.

I am not writing that to scare anyone off. Understood properly, this is one of the strongest tools available to a Florida buyer whose income comfortably supports a monthly payment but whose savings do not yet cover the cash it takes to get to the closing table. Misunderstood, it turns into an unpleasant surprise years later when you sell or try to refinance.

The occupational list is worth reading as a statement of intent. Florida wrote this around the jobs that keep a county functioning day to day, the people who staff the hospital, the school, the fire station, and the child care center. In a market where housing costs have moved faster than public sector and health care pay, that is the gap the program was built to close.

How the Deferred Second Mortgage Works

Take the phrase apart and it stops being intimidating.

Zero percent means no interest accrues. The balance you repay is the balance you received, however many years later that turns out to be. In a state where insurance and taxes have both been moving in one direction, an obligation that charges no interest and never grows is genuinely valuable.

Non-amortizing means the balance does not shrink either. You are not chipping away at it every month, because there is no monthly payment on it at all.

Thirty-year deferred means it sits quietly behind your first mortgage instead of showing up in your payment.

Second mortgage means exactly what it says. It is recorded against the property, in second position behind your primary loan. It is a lien on your house, not a gift certificate.

So when does it come due? Florida Housing is specific: in full, upon sale of the property, refinancing of the first mortgage, transfer of the deed, or if you no longer occupy the home as your primary residence.

Read the refinance trigger twice, because that is the one that catches people. Rates move. Five years from now you may very much want to refinance your first mortgage into a better one, and doing that triggers repayment of the second. Whether any accommodation exists in that situation is a question for Florida Housing and your lender at the time, not something a web page should be answering for you. What you should take away is simple: the assistance is a claim against your future equity, and the moment you touch the first mortgage, that claim comes to the front of the line.

The practical version. If you buy, stay, and sell in year twelve with real appreciation behind you, the payoff comes out of your proceeds and you barely feel it. If you buy and sell in year three in a flat market, that same payoff can eat a meaningful share of whatever equity you built. Neither outcome is hidden. Both are knowable before you sign.

This is a different shape of help than a USDA loan, which is the other financing question Marion County buyers bring me most. USDA solves the down payment by not requiring one. Hometown Heroes hands you money for the down payment and closing costs and records a lien for it. One removes the requirement, the other funds it. There is a full USDA page linked at the bottom of this one if that comparison matters for where you are shopping.

On stacking, since the assistance is a second, it sits behind a first mortgage originated by a participating lender. Which first mortgage products it can pair with is set by Florida Housing and administered through its approved lender network, and those details can differ between funding cycles. Ask a participating lender what pairings are approved for the round you are applying under instead of trusting the structure described in an article written two cycles ago.

The mechanics of the reservation itself are worth knowing before you talk to a lender. A participating lender reserves the assistance for you through a system called eHousingPlus, at the point your first mortgage rate locks, not before. From there you have 60 days to reach underwriter certification, or the reservation cancels automatically and the money goes back into the pool for someone else. The lender also needs to see a minimum 640 credit score and confirm you have completed FHFC-approved homebuyer education before that reservation can be made. If you are buying new construction, the home needs its certificate of occupancy before a reservation can be placed on it. The program pairs with FHA, VA, RD, and Conventional first mortgages. None of this is something I control or verify. The lender is the one who actually qualifies you, and I hand off to one rather than guessing on your behalf.

Who Qualifies, by Job and by Ownership History

Florida Housing limits the program to borrowers employed full-time by a Florida-based employer as a health care worker, school staff member, first responder, public safety or court employee, or child care worker. Military service members and reserves are eligible, as are veterans employed full-time by a Florida-based employer.

Two words in that sentence do more filtering than the job titles do.

Full-time. Part-time work in a qualifying role is a different conversation, and it is one to have with a lender before you build a plan around the program.

Florida-based employer. Florida has a lot of people living here on paychecks issued somewhere else. If you work remotely for a company headquartered in another state, do not assume you are in and do not assume you are out. Ask directly, early, and get the answer from someone who reserves these funds for a living.

On the occupational categories themselves, the language is broader than the job title most people picture. School staff member is not a synonym for classroom teacher. A hospital employs far more people than the ones wearing scrubs. If your role sits inside a qualifying institution but is not the obvious face of it, that is worth asking about rather than quietly ruling yourself out over.

Then there is the first-time buyer requirement, which surprises more people than any other part of this program.

Housing finance programs of this type generally treat you as a first-time buyer if you have not owned and occupied a home as your primary residence in the previous three years. I am giving you that as the general standard rather than as a verdict on your situation, and the hedge is deliberate. The definition gets applied by a lender against your actual ownership history, and the edge cases are where it gets settled.

Real situations that turn on details: you inherited a house you never lived in. You own a rental you have never occupied. You divorced and the home went to your former spouse. Every one of those has a real answer, and none of those answers should come from a page like this one. They come from a participating lender reading your file.

If you have served, ask specifically whether the first-time requirement applies to you the same way. Do not assume in either direction based on something you read online, this page included.

The useful takeaway is that plenty of people who have owned a home before still qualify, and a lot of them never find out because they read the words first-time buyer and stopped.

Income and Price Limits, and Why No Numbers Appear Here

There is a ceiling on household income and a ceiling on purchase price. Both are set by county. I am not printing either figure on this page, and I want to explain why rather than leave you wondering whether I just did not look it up.

County limits get republished. A page that quotes a Marion County income cap accurately today reads as authoritative and is quietly wrong eighteen months from now, by which point it has been indexed, linked, and screenshotted into somebody's notes. A buyer who talks themselves out of a program because of a stale number I published is a far worse outcome than a buyer who has to make one phone call to get a current one.

Where the real figures live: Florida Housing publishes income and purchase price limits by county on its own site, and any participating lender can pull the current numbers for your county and household size in about the time it takes to open a file. Ask for both, and ask for them in writing.

Two things worth understanding before you make that call.

County variation here is not a rounding difference. Florida spans counties with wildly different income and price profiles, and the ceiling governing a purchase in Marion County is not the ceiling governing one in Broward or Monroe. If your search crosses a county line, you may be working against two different sets of limits at once, which is a real planning problem and not a technicality.

The second thing matters more. Do not disqualify yourself on a mental estimate. Whose income counts, which income counts, and what gets deducted before the comparison is made are all technical questions with real answers, and the gross number on your pay stub is rarely the figure that decides it. People rule themselves out of assistance programs in their own heads, on arithmetic they did in the car, and never make the call that would have corrected them. That is a bad way to lose something you were entitled to.

The purchase price cap deserves the same treatment for a different reason. It shapes what is worth touring. Finding out about it after you have fallen for a house is how a promising Saturday turns into a wasted month.

First Come, First Served, and What That Really Means

This is the part of the program that decides outcomes, and it is the part almost every article about it gets quietly wrong by the time you find it.

Hometown Heroes funding arrives in cycles. The Legislature appropriates money, Florida Housing makes it available, participating lenders reserve it for individual buyers as loans get locked, and when a round is committed, it is committed. First come, first served, with the reservations happening at lender level in real time.

What the record shows about the pace. The 2023 round of $100 million was fully committed in under three weeks. The prior 2025-2026 round opened August 18, 2025 with $50 million plus $13 million in recycled funds, and took about six months to fully commit. The current round relaunched July 13, 2026 with a new $50 million allocation. Florida Housing's own board materials for its September 25, 2026 meeting show roughly 1,159 loans in the pipeline using just over $18 million of that money, based on data as of September 10, 2026. That leaves somewhere around $32 million still uncommitted as of this writing, and at that pace the round could run through the rest of the year before it is fully claimed. I want to be honest about what that number is and is not. It is my own arithmetic off a public board packet, not a live balance Florida Housing publishes anywhere, and it will be stale the moment a lender in another county locks a batch of loans. Treat it as evidence the round moves slower than 2023 did, not as a promise about what is left when you call.

Line those up and the conclusion writes itself. These rounds do not sit open for months waiting for you to get organized. A round can be fully claimed before most eligible buyers in the state have heard that it opened.

So I am going to do something unusual for a real estate page and decline to tell you whether money is available as you read this. I have no idea when you are reading it. This page might be a day old or three years old, and the honest answer changes by the week. Availability shifts without notice, nobody announces it to you personally, and the only two sources worth trusting are Florida Housing Finance Corporation directly and a participating lender who can see reservation status today. Any other answer, including a confident one, is a guess wearing a suit.

That reality should change your strategy completely. When a program gets claimed in weeks, the buyers who land it are not the ones who start when it opens. They are the ones who finished the slow parts months earlier and only had to make one call.

Being ready looks like this.

Pre-approved with a lender who participates in Florida Housing's programs, not just any lender who answered the phone. Not every loan officer works these files, and hunting for one after a round opens burns the days that actually mattered.

Documentation gathered in advance. Verification of full-time employment in a qualifying role, income history, and a clear answer on your ownership history for the last three years.

Your county's current income and purchase price limits confirmed in writing.

Homebuyer education handled if the round requires it. Ask whether it does, because a course you have not taken is a poor thing to discover on a deadline.

A property you can go under contract on, or at minimum a search narrow enough that you could move within days.

If you check and a round is closed, that is information, not a verdict. Appropriations have come back in past cycles. Getting your file assembled during a closed stretch costs you nothing and puts you near the front when funding returns. One thing I will say flatly: never structure a purchase around assistance nobody has confirmed in writing is reserved for you. Contracts have dates. Funding rounds do not care about them.

How I Get Paid on a Hometown Heroes Deal

Buyers ask me this less than they should, so I am putting it on the page unprompted.

Realtor commission cannot be paid out of the down payment assistance funds themselves. On top of that, Florida Housing's current lender guide for this program caps any realtor fee you pay directly at $500. That is not a typo and it is not a negotiable line item I can talk a lender into changing.

What that means in practice is that my compensation on an HTH purchase comes from seller concessions built into the offer, or from a cooperative fee offered on the listing side, not from your pocket. The buyer-broker agreement we sign has to be written to reflect that from the start, not adjusted after the fact.

I am telling you this for the same reason I do not print stale income limits on this page. I would rather you know exactly how the money moves before you sign anything than have you find out at the closing table.

Step two. I connect you with a lender who actually participates in Florida Housing's programs, and they run your pre-qualification. Step three. Once you are pre-qualified, we tour homes in Ocala or Marion Oaks that fit your price range and the program's purchase price limit for your county. From there it is a normal purchase, just one with a second mortgage attached that you now understand.

When This Is the Wrong Tool, and Where I Fit

I would rather talk a buyer out of chasing this program in week one than watch a deal built on it fall apart in week six. Here is when it is not the answer.

Your occupation is not on the list. That one is binary, and there is no creative way around it. Florida Housing runs other homebuyer assistance programs that are not gated on occupation, so the right move is to ask a participating lender what else you qualify for rather than treating this as your only shot.

You need to move soon and the round is closed. Waiting on a future appropriation is not a timeline. If you have a lease ending or a job starting, buy on financing that exists today.

You expect to sell or refinance quickly. The second mortgage comes due on both events. If your honest guess is that you are out of this house in two or three years, run the payoff against your likely equity before you take the assistance, not after.

You are buying an investment property or a second home. Occupancy as your primary residence is a condition of the deferral, so the moment the property stops being where you live, the balance is due.

You do not understand what you are signing. Not knowing is fine and normal. Signing anyway is the problem. Ask until the lien, the payoff triggers, and your county's limits are all clear to you in plain language.

And if you are buying in southwest Marion County specifically, compare this against USDA before you commit to either. They solve different problems, they are not automatically interchangeable, and the right one depends on your address, your household, and how long you plan to stay. Have someone put both against your actual file rather than picking whichever one you heard about first.
