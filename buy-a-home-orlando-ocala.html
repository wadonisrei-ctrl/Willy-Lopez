What I Do for Buyers

I am a licensed real estate agent based in Central Florida. I work with buyers in the Greater Orlando area and in Ocala, helping them find a house that fits their budget, their timeline, and the way they actually live.

Two markets, two very different conversations. Orlando is a large metro where the main problem is narrowing down. Ocala is a smaller county where the main problem is understanding what you are looking at, because the housing stock varies wildly street to street.

A lot of buyers find me through the rental side of my work. If I helped you find a place to rent and you are now thinking about owning, you do not have to start over with a stranger.

My job is to make sure you understand what you are getting into before you sign anything. That means explaining how the process runs in Florida specifically, what it actually costs to own in different parts of Central Florida, and which areas match what you say you want.

Orlando vs Ocala: Which Market Is Right for You?

These are not two versions of the same market, and picking the wrong one costs more than picking the wrong house inside the right one.

Orlando gives you a large job market, an international airport, and a genuine range of neighborhoods across four counties. It also costs more, and the metro is wide enough that where you buy determines how you spend your weeknights. Competition for well-priced homes in the desirable school zones is still real even in a calmer market.

Ocala sits about 90 minutes north and remains one of the more affordable places to buy in Florida. It suits remote workers, retirees, healthcare staff at the local hospitals, and anyone who wants more house and more lot than the metro will give them. The tradeoff is straightforward: a smaller job market and a drive to Orlando that nobody wants to make daily.

The question I ask first is not what you want to spend. It is where you have to be on a Tuesday morning. That answer usually settles the market before we ever talk about houses.

Still torn? Say where you have to be during the week and what you are working with, and I will argue both sides, including the case against whichever one you are already leaning toward.

What Working With Me Actually Looks Like

Here is the sequence, so there are no mysteries.

We start with a conversation. Where you need to be, what you are working with, what your timeline looks like, and what you are not willing to give up. This is also where I tell you if I think the plan needs adjusting before you spend money on anything.

Next, you get pre-approved. Not pre-qualified, which is a guess. Pre-approved, where a lender has actually verified income, assets, and credit. Everything downstream is more accurate once that number is real, and sellers in both markets expect the letter with any offer.

Then we narrow the map. Two or three areas, not the whole region. This is the step buyers most want to skip and the one that saves the most time.

Then the search loop: I send you properties worth your attention rather than everything that technically matches, we tour or walk through on video, and after each one we adjust the criteria based on what you actually reacted to instead of what you told me at the start.

When you find one, I pull comparable sales, tell you what I think it is worth, and we structure the offer around your priorities. Under contract, I keep inspections, the insurance quote, the appraisal, the lender, and the title company all moving in the right order, because in Florida the insurance answer can change the deal and you want it early.

Throughout, I answer the phone. It is a low bar and it is not universally cleared.

Out-of-State Buyers

A large share of Central Florida buyers are moving in from somewhere else. New York, New Jersey, Chicago, and South Florida come up most often.

Buying from a distance is not just buying with less information. It is buying with information that has been curated for you by people who want the sale. Listing photos are shot to flatter. A neighborhood looks fine on a map and different at 6pm on a weekday. Nothing in the listing tells you the roof is at the age where insurers start asking questions.

So the job changes. I walk properties and narrate what I am seeing, including the parts nobody photographs: the drainage, the condition of the neighboring houses, the road noise, what the block feels like. I take video that is honest rather than flattering. And I will tell you when something is a no before you spend a plane ticket on it.

That is the part that matters most for a remote purchase. You are not paying attention to who is finding you houses. You are relying on someone to tell you the truth about them when you cannot see for yourself.

What It Costs to Get to the Closing Table

The down payment gets all the attention and it is not the only money you need. Budget for the rest early so nothing lands on you three days before closing.

Earnest money goes in when your offer is accepted. It is credited back to you at closing, so it is not spent, but it does leave your account well before you get keys.

Inspections come out of pocket, before you know whether the deal survives. That feels bad and it is still the best money you will spend in the entire transaction.

The appraisal is usually collected by your lender, sometimes upfront.

Closing costs cover title work, lender fees, recording, survey if you order one, and state taxes on the mortgage note. Plan on a few percent of the purchase price on top of your down payment.

Prepaids and escrow are the line that surprises people relocating from other states. Your lender collects your first year of homeowners insurance plus a cushion of taxes and insurance at closing, and because the insurance piece is heavier in Florida, this line runs heavier here too.

One more that is not a closing cost and belongs in the same conversation: keep a reserve after you close. Florida ages roofs, air conditioners, and water heaters faster than most states, and buying with nothing left in the account is how a manageable repair turns into a crisis.
