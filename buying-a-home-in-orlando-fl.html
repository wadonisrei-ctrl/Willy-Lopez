Guide

Where the Orlando Market Stands Right Now

Per the Orlando Regional Realtor Association's monthly market reports, the metro median sale price was $385,000 in March 2026, up modestly year over year after the rapid appreciation of 2021 to 2023. Conditions have shifted toward balance: more inventory on the market, homes taking longer to sell, and far less of the multiple-offer chaos that defined the pandemic-era market.

That median hides more than it reveals, though. Orlando is a wide metro spanning Orange, Osceola, Seminole, and Lake counties, and the spread between the cheapest and priciest submarkets is enormous. A number that describes the whole metro describes almost none of it accurately.

For a buyer, the spread is the opportunity. Picking the right submarket for your budget and your commute matters more here than it would in a smaller, more uniform market, and it is the single decision that most changes what you can afford.

Orlando Neighborhoods Worth Knowing Before You Buy

The metro sorts into rough tiers, and knowing where each one sits saves you months of aimless searching.

Kissimmee, Celebration, and the Davenport side of Polk County are the accessible end. More house for the money, quick access to the attractions corridor, and a lot of hospitality workers who need to be close to it.

Lake Nona in southeast Orlando prices above the metro median. The Medical City healthcare corridor drives it, along with a large supply of newer construction in master-planned neighborhoods. Strong fit for healthcare workers and anyone who wants a house nothing has gone wrong with yet.

Winter Garden, Horizon West, and Oviedo are where families land when school zoning is the priority. Newer four-bedroom stock, deed restrictions almost everywhere, and prices that reflect the demand.

Winter Park runs higher and sells something different: established streets, a walkable core, actual tree canopy, and proximity to downtown. Buyers choose it over new construction on purpose.

Dr. Phillips and Windermere sit at the top of the metro, especially near the water or behind a gate.

Those tiers move. What does not move is the logic behind them: employer proximity, school zoning, and how new the housing stock is. Tell me your commute, your budget, and what you actually want out of the house, and I will tell you which tier belongs on your list.

Florida Rules That Change the Math on an Orlando Purchase

A few Florida rules shift the numbers on a purchase here compared to buying in another state.

Property taxes come with a break for owner-occupants, and it is worth understanding the structure rather than a rumored dollar amount. Per the Florida Department of Revenue, the first $25,000 of the homestead exemption applies to all property taxes including school district levies, and an additional exemption applies to assessed value above $50,000 for every taxing authority except school districts. Once homesteaded, the Save Our Homes cap limits how fast your assessment can climb, to 3% a year or the change in the consumer price index, whichever is lower. In submarkets that appreciate quickly, that cap compounds into real money the longer you stay put.

Insurance is the number that surprises Orlando buyers most. Florida's homeowners market is one of the hardest in the country, strained by hurricane losses and insurer pullouts, and premiums reflect that even well inland in Orange County. Get a quote during your inspection period, not after closing, and build it into the monthly payment you are testing against your budget rather than treating it as a rounding error.

Most newer Central Florida communities carry HOA fees, and the master-planned areas are almost always deed-restricted. Before you write an offer, find out what the association charges, what it covers, and whether its budget is healthy.

In my experience, plan on roughly a month to six weeks from accepted offer to keys on a financed purchase, though your lender's own timeline is the one that governs it. Cash can move faster.

How I Work an Orlando Search

In a balanced market the pressure comes off the calendar and lands on the analysis. Nobody is forcing you to decide in four hours anymore. That means the question stops being how fast you can write and starts being whether the house is priced correctly and whether you are buying in the right place.

So the first thing I do is narrow the map. Buyers arrive wanting to see everything across four counties, and touring across the metro at random burns weekends and teaches you very little. Pinning down two or three submarkets that genuinely fit your budget and your commute makes every showing after that useful.

Then the pricing work. When homes are sitting longer, list price and market value drift apart, and a house that has been available for months is telling you something. I read days on market, price history, and comparable sales together before you get attached to anything.

On the offer itself, a slower market gives you room most buyers do not use. Inspection terms, closing timeline, repair credits, and a seller contribution toward closing costs are all live. Structuring those well is often worth more than shaving a few thousand off the price.

And I will tell you when a house is wrong for you. There is no version of this job where that is not the most valuable thing I bring.

Who Buys Homes in Orlando (and Why)

The Orlando buyer market is broad, but a few segments show up over and over.

Out-of-state relocators from the Northeast, Midwest, and Miami make up a large share. No state income tax and a lower cost of living than the coasts are the consistent draw, even with Orlando's own prices well above where they sat five years ago.

Healthcare workers and researchers gravitate toward the Medical City corridor, where being close to the job outweighs paying more for the house.

Families weight school zoning above almost everything else, which pulls them toward Winter Garden, Horizon West, and Oviedo even when closer-in alternatives would cost less.

Hospitality and attractions workers look south and west, toward Kissimmee, Celebration, and Davenport, where the commute to the theme park corridor is short and the entry price is lower.

And there is a quieter group worth naming: renters who have been in Orlando a few years, watched their rent reset at every renewal, and decided they would rather own the payment. That crossover is a bigger share of the buyer pool than most market write-ups acknowledge.
